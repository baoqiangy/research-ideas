Comparative Evaluation of Organizational Digital Forensic Readiness Frameworks

Step-by-step master's project guideline • CS597

Revised scope: comparative research and recommendations • Source discovery checked September 18, 2026

Project Goal

Conduct a structured, reproducible comparison of existing frameworks that help organizations prepare for digital forensic investigations. Identify what each framework addresses, how it can be applied, what evidence supports it, and which organizational contexts it suits. Use the findings to identify research gaps and requirements for possible future framework development.

Primary research question: How do existing digital forensic readiness frameworks differ in organizational and technical coverage, practical applicability, assessment support, and strength of validation, and what do these differences imply for organizations choosing an approach?
  • RQ1: Which readiness capabilities and organizational responsibilities does each framework explicitly address?
  • RQ2: How actionable is its guidance, and does it provide a way to assess or improve readiness?
  • RQ3: How well does its documented guidance support common organizational scenarios and constraints?
  • RQ4: What validation has been reported, what limitations remain, and which gaps justify future research?
Scope change: The required contribution is an evidence-backed comparative study. Developing a new readiness framework, assigning organizations a new maturity score, implementing blockchain, building a logging laboratory, and measuring forensic recovery performance are no longer required. A comparison codebook is a research instrument, not a newly validated readiness framework.

Retain evidence integrity, human and organizational readiness, and behavioral visibility from the original project as comparison themes. Broaden the analysis to include governance, legal and privacy considerations, implementation requirements, assessment support, and validation.

Research Roadmap

  1. Define scope and write the review protocol
  2. Search and select the literature
  3. Confirm the framework shortlist and source access
  4. Extract framework descriptions and validation evidence
  5. Create and pilot the comparison codebook
  6. Code and compare the frameworks
  7. Apply common organizational scenarios
  8. Check consistency and robustness
  9. Synthesize findings and future research
  10. Submit the report and reproducibility materials
Step 1

Define Scope and Write the Review Protocol

Use this working definition: organizational digital forensic readiness is the preparation of people, processes, technology, and governance to make relevant digital evidence available and usable when an investigation is needed.

Make the framework family the unit of comparison. Link original proposals, revisions, and validation papers; do not count a follow-up paper as an independent framework. Distinguish organizational readiness frameworks, implementation processes, technical architectures, and broader standards or guidance.

Target four or five core framework families, plus one or two supporting guidance documents. Study enterprise IT and cloud-using organizations; specialized IoT, mobile, or industrial approaches are optional extensions only when justified by the selected context.

Before screening, record the research questions, databases, exact search strings, search dates, eligibility rules, planned extraction fields, comparison procedure, and limitations. Search from database inception to the actual final search date. Keep a dated amendment log when the protocol changes.

Output: A two- to three-page review protocol with a feasible scope agreed with the supervisor.
Step 2

Search and Select the Literature

Search available academic databases such as Scopus or Web of Science, IEEE Xplore, and ACM Digital Library. Supplement with Google Scholar, publisher sites, institutional repositories, and official standards sites. Document unavailable databases rather than claiming to have searched them.

("digital forensic readiness" OR "forensic readiness" OR
 "organizational forensic readiness" OR "organisational forensic readiness")
AND (framework OR model OR process OR maturity OR assessment)

Supplementary search:
("cloud forensic readiness" OR "network forensic readiness")
AND (organization OR organisation OR enterprise OR framework)

Adapt syntax to each database. Keep the broad search so the organizational qualifier does not hide relevant work. Examine references and citing papers for every included framework, including newer revisions and validation studies.

IncludeExclude from the core comparison
Primary publications proposing or substantively refining a structured readiness approach with organizational relevance and identifiable components.Purely post-incident investigation models without substantive preparation guidance; isolated forensic tools; unrelated cybersecurity maturity models.
Full texts sufficiently detailed for extraction; official standards with accessible relevant clauses; documented validation studies.Duplicate versions; promotional descriptions; abstract-only records for detailed coding. Retain inaccessible candidates in the screening log.

Screen titles and abstracts, then full texts. Record exclusion reasons at full-text stage, deduplicate records, and report counts at each stage. State any language restrictions. Reviews can locate candidates and gaps, but primary sources should support framework coding. Describe this as a structured comparative review unless the completed search and screening support a stronger systematic-review claim.

Output: Search log, bibliography, selection flow, and inclusion/exclusion register. The seed list below is a starting point, not the complete search result.

Step 4

Extract Framework Descriptions and Evidence

Create a one- to two-page profile for each family before comparing it. Preserve the authors' original terminology, then map equivalent concepts to common labels. Keep mappings traceable; similar names do not necessarily mean equivalent capabilities.

Extraction groupRequired fields
Identity and purposeFamily ID; authors; title; year/edition; DOI/URL; intended users; organizational setting; readiness definition and objectives.
StructureDimensions, components, processes, relationships, lifecycle stages, and scope boundaries.
ApplicationPrescribed actions; responsible roles; prerequisites; required resources; expected artifacts; assessment or improvement procedure.
ValidationConceptual argument, expert review, case study, prototype, or field evaluation; sample/context; method; outcomes; limitations. Record “not reported” where appropriate.
TraceabilityExact page/section/table/figure; concise paraphrase; coding rationale; uncertainty; whether the statement is the authors' claim or your inference.

Separate validation of a framework's relevance or clarity from evidence of improved investigation outcomes. Expert agreement does not establish reductions in investigation cost or evidence loss. Publication age and citation count are contextual information, not quality scores.

Output: Framework profiles and an evidence extraction table.
Step 5

Create and Pilot the Comparison Codebook

Start with the dimensions below, derive concrete questions from the literature, and pilot them on two contrasting frameworks. Aim for approximately 18–24 focused questions. Define each question, inclusion boundaries, evidence required, and examples before coding the whole set. Document criteria added or revised during the pilot.

DimensionExample comparison questions
Governance and objectivesAre readiness objectives connected to business needs? Are ownership, management support, and resources addressed?
People and proceduresAre roles, training, reporting, and escalation specified? Are evidence-preservation responsibilities clear?
Evidence lifecycle and integrityAre evidence sources, collection, retention, handling, provenance, and integrity protection addressed?
Activity visibilityDoes guidance support reconstructing relevant user and system activity, including attribution and time correlation?
Legal and privacy considerationsAre authorization, privacy, jurisdiction, and legal coordination considered? Evaluate the framework's treatment; do not make a legal-compliance determination.
Implementation and contextAre prerequisites, artifacts, and actionable procedures given? How are provider dependencies and resource constraints handled?
Assessment and improvementDoes it supply an assessment procedure, indicators, a checklist, or a feedback cycle? Distinguish authors' instruments from student-created ones.
Validation evidenceWhat was evaluated, in which settings, and with what results? Which claims remain untested?

Use descriptive codes rather than an organizational maturity score

CodeMeaning
A — AbsentNo relevant provision found after reviewing the accessible full source within its stated scope.
M — MentionedThe capability is explicitly identified, but implementation detail is limited.
O — Operational guidanceThe source explains actions, procedures, responsibilities, or artifacts sufficiently to guide application. Define criterion-specific anchors in the codebook.
U — Unclear / unavailableInsufficient access or ambiguous evidence prevents a reliable judgment. Record which reason applies.
N/A — Outside declared scopeThe criterion does not apply to the framework's stated purpose; justify this explicitly. Report the resulting limit on suitability for broader contexts.

Record validation evidence separately from coverage. A detailed prescription can be untested; a concept can have expert support without operational instructions. Do not force these properties into one score.

Output: Versioned codebook and pilot coding with documented revisions.
Step 6

Code and Compare the Frameworks

For each criterion–framework pair, enter the code, source locator, supporting paraphrase, rationale, and uncertainty. Keep a separate claim-to-source table so every substantive finding can be audited.

Framework_ID | Version | Criterion_ID | Code | Page_or_section
Evidence_paraphrase | Rationale | Uncertainty | Coder | Coding_date

Produce a criterion-by-framework matrix and a dimension-level summary. Discuss differences in purpose and abstraction before comparing breadth. Report how many entries are assessable and how many are U or N/A. If summarizing proportions, state the denominator and show the underlying counts; coverage is not an effectiveness or organizational-readiness percentage.

Avoid a universal winner or arbitrary weighted total. Identify tradeoffs: breadth, actionable detail, context fit, and evidential support may point to different choices. A missing capability is a gap relative to a specified organizational need, not automatically a defect in a narrowly scoped framework.

Output: Completed comparison matrix, source traceability, and a narrative answering RQ1–RQ2.
Step 7

Apply Common Organizational Scenarios

Conduct structured desk-based walkthroughs using the same scenario facts and questions for every framework. Prepare the scenario packets before applying the frameworks. The following are synthetic research cases, not empirical observations.

ScenarioFixed organizational factsQuestions to examine
S1. Resource-constrained SME80 staff; two IT generalists; outsourced incident support; local authentication logs retained for seven days; no assigned evidence custodian. An account is suspected of misuse after ten days.What preparation does the framework prescribe for retention, ownership, reporting, and access to expertise? Which supporting artifacts would be needed?
S2. Enterprise insider incident2,000 staff; central logging; privileged administrators; sensitive shared files; monitoring permissions unclear; an administrator resets an account before escalation.Does the guidance address attribution, evidence preservation, authorized monitoring, and coordination between IT, management, HR, and legal functions?
S3. Cloud-dependent organizationAn organization operates IaaS workloads across two regions; provider-managed logs have limited retention; its contract has no specified evidence-export response time. A compromised workload is deleted.Does the framework guide preparation for provider cooperation, evidence access, retention, contractual dependencies, and jurisdictional issues?
  1. List the readiness needs implied by each scenario independently of any one framework.
  2. Identify source-supported actions each framework would guide the organization to take before an incident.
  3. Record the artifacts an organization would need to produce, such as a retention policy or responsibility assignment. Label artifacts inferred by the student.
  4. Identify unresolved decisions, assumptions, and gaps requiring supplementary guidance.
  5. Write a short suitability judgment supported by source references and scenario facts.

Use “direct guidance,” “requires interpretation,” “no guidance found,” or “not assessable” for scenario judgments. Do not invent evidence recovery rates, investigation times, cost savings, or successful outcomes. These walkthroughs evaluate documented applicability, not real-world effectiveness.

Output: Three scenario packets, completed walkthrough records, and a suitability comparison answering RQ3.
Step 8

Check Consistency and Robustness

  • Coding consistency: Ask a supervisor or second reader, if available, to independently code a varied sample of approximately 20% of entries. Compare initial decisions before resolving disagreements; report the selection method, agreement counts, and changes.
  • Single-researcher fallback: Recode a sample after an interval without consulting the original decisions. Report this as an intra-rater check and acknowledge the absence of independent coding.
  • Source audit: Verify every major conclusion and a sample of remaining cells against source passages.
  • Robustness: Check whether recommendations change when uncertain entries are left unresolved versus plausible alternative interpretations, when specialist approaches are analyzed separately, or when only accessible full texts are used.
  • Limits: Discuss search and language bias, missing full texts, unequal publication detail, framework version differences, subjective interpretation, and synthetic scenario limitations.

Expert feedback is optional and is not a substitute for source evidence. If collecting practitioner interviews or survey responses as research, obtain the university's applicable ethics determination before recruitment or data collection.

Output: Consistency log, sensitivity discussion, and threats-to-validity section.
Step 9

Synthesize Findings and Define Future Work

Answer each research question explicitly. Recommend approaches for stated contexts rather than declaring one framework universally best. Separate what the sources demonstrate, what your comparison indicates, and what remains unknown.

For each proposed gap, provide: the organizational need, relevant comparison results, supporting source locations, the consequence for that context, and a future research question. Distinguish a documentation gap, a capability gap, and a lack of validation. Check newer literature before calling a gap unresolved.

Possible future work includes developing a combined framework only if the comparison demonstrates a need, operationalizing assessment indicators, evaluating organizational deployments, or testing evidence-preservation mechanisms in a laboratory. Specify what would need validation. Framework construction, a new scoring model, and blockchain experiments belong in a later project rather than the required deliverables here.

Output: Context-specific recommendations and a prioritized future-research agenda answering RQ4.
